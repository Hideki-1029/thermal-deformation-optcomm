This paper investigates the thermal drift of the line-of-sight (LOS) resulting from temperature fluctuations dur-
ing the on-orbit operation of a large-aperture, long-focal-length off-axis reflective star tracker. It aims to develop
optimization strategies to improve thermal stability and LOS accuracy. A tolerance analysis of the optical system
was first performed to quantify the impact of each optical component on LOS variation, with a specific focus on
the primary, secondary, and tertiary mirrors. Subsequently, an LOS sensitivity matrix was constructed to corre-
late structural displacements with optical image point deviations. Based on this, the flexible support structures
of the mirrors were sequentially optimized, resulting in a configuration that effectively reduces thermal drift.
The results indicate that, following optimization, the LOS thermal drift angle decreased from 0.03499” /°C to
0.02435” /°C under a 5°C temperature increase, yielding a 30.4% reduction. The measured LOS thermal drift
angle was 0.02641” /°C, deviating by only 8.46% from the simulation result. Surface figure measurements showed
that the respective surface errors of the primary, secondary, and tertiary mirrors were 0.0152, 0.0172, and 0.0144,
all satisfying the star tracker’s technical specification (RMS < 1/50). Wavefront aberration measurements yielded
RMS values of 0.0662, 0.0682, 0.0732, 0.0722, and 0.0692 across five fields of view, conforming to the imaging
system’s technical specification (RMS < 1/14). The proposed optimization strategy enhances thermal stability,
mitigates LOS thermal drift, and ensures that the final design fulfills the high-precision attitude determination

‘The optical axis drift induced by temperature fluctuations

was limited to constant-temperature conditions and did not
provide quantitative data to isolate the thermal effects associated
with the star tracker bracket. Zhong et al. [10] conducted a
detailed analysis using on-orbit telemetry data from a remote
sensing satellite to evaluate the thermal deformation character-
istics of the star tracker structure and successfully constructed a
corresponding thermal deformation model.

‘This paper discusses the optical axis errors caused by thermal
effects during the on-orbit operation of star trackers. First, a
tolerance analysis is conducted to quantify the influence of each
optical component on the optical axis. Next, the sensitivity of
the six degrees of freedom displacement for each optical compo-
nent to variations in the optical axis is calculated using optical
design software. The supporting structure is then optimized
through software co-simulation. The results from the sensitivity
analysis are utilized to correlate structural data with the param-
eters of the optical model. Finally, the validity of the theoretical
design is confirmed by comparing the simulated and measured
values of the optical axis thermal drift angle, the root mean
square (RMS) values of the surface figure errors of each optical
component, and the RMS value of the wavefront aberration for
the entire system.

B. Tolerance Analysis

Tolerance analysis primarily involves defining the types and
magnitudes of errors within an optical system, simulating the
effects of manufacturing and assembly errors on optical per-
formance. This approach enables the prediction of performance
limits post-assembly, ensuring system manufacturability while
striving to achieve optimal quality at minimal cost [11]. Since
the optical system described in this paper consists of multi-
ple components, alterations in any individual component
can impact the overall system performance. Consequently,
this study identifies the optical components that significantly
affect the optical axis and optimizes them to stabilize their
performance.

‘This study utilizes ZEMAX software for tolerance analysis.
Since this research focuses exclusively on the structural opti-
mization of the optical components, the preceding optical
optimization is excluded in this work. In ZEMAX, the tolerance
analysis mode is configured to sensitivity analysis, with the
aiming error serving as an approximate substitute for evaluating
the optical system’s line-of-sight variation tolerance. The aiming
errorin ZEMAX represents the displacement between the actual
position of the spot or chief ray on the image plane and the ideal
position, caused by image plane tile or optical axis misalign-
ment. The aiming error is defined as the change in the radial
coordinate of the chief ray in the on-axis field of view divided
by the effective focal length. The change in the line-of-sight
angle is the change in the coordinate of the chief ray divided by
the effective focal length. Therefore, the aiming error should
be the sine of the change in the line-of-sight angle. Since the
angle changes involved in this study are at the arcsecond level,
which are small-angle changes, the aiming crror can be used to
approximate the change in the line-of-sight angle.

Following the tolerance analysis, particular attention was
directed toward the worst-case deviations, with results presented
in Table 2. Among them, TETX and TETY are the tilt toler-

ances of the component in the X and Y directions, respectively,

demonstrate that surfaces 3, 8, and 4 have a significant impact
on the aiming error. The correlation of the lens data with the
system layout in ZEMAX revealed that the identified surfaces
correspond to the primary mirror, tertiary mirror, and secondary
mirror, respectively. The tolerance analysis indicates that the
optical components most significantly affecting line-of-sight
stability are the primary, tertiary, and secondary mirrors.

C. Line-of-Sight Sensitivity Analysis

Based on the above tolerance analysis, the research focus of
this study has shifted from global optimization of the entire
optical system to targeted optimization of the primary, tertiary,
and secondary mirrors. However, as the six degrees of freedom
(6-DOFs) displacements of the optical components all influ-
ence line-of-sight (LOS) variation, it is necessary to compute
the sensitivity matrix relating the 6-DOF motions of these
three mirrors to LOS deviations. Figure 3 intuitively shows
the relationship between the six degrees of freedom (6-DOFs)
displacements and the line-of-sight (LOS) deviations. When
external conditions such as temperature change, the flexible sup-
ports of the mirrors will produce displacements, thereby causing
the 6-DOF displacements of the mirrors. These displacements
are intuitively manifested as changes in the position of the
mirrors, that is, from the black solid line position to the red
dashed line position in Fig, 3. This ultimately leads to changes
in the position of the image point after the light passes through
the system and reaches the image plane, which means that the
line-of-sight has changed.

Neglecting higher-order effects, the optical system can be
approximated using a first-order Taylor expansion of the optical
path matrix, which describes the propagation of rays from the
entrance pupil through the system to the static reference surface
at the exit pupil [12]:

mirrors spatial location cannot be preserved consistently in an
under-constrained configuration, while an over-constrained
design may result in elastic deformation. Consequently, in the
design of the support scheme, it is imperative first to ascertain
the precise complete constraint of the mirror and then proceed
with the structural design of the flexible support [14].

C. Wavefront Aberration Detection

‘The principle of wavefront aberration detection involves assess-
ing the imaging quality of an optical system by measuring the
deviation between the actual wavefront and the ideal wave-
front. In this study, measured wavefront aberrations are used
to evaluate the effectiveness of the flexible structural design of
the mirror assembly. An interferometer and a reference mirror
are employed to detect the system's wavefront aberrations. The
wavefront aberration measurement setup is shown in Fig. 15.

‘The primary, secondary, tertiary, and folding mirrors, used
to redirect the optical path, are installed in the optical frame
according to their designated spatial positions. A standard mir-
ror is positioned at the entrance pupil, while the interferometer
is located at the exit pupil. A laser beam emitted from the inter-
ferometer enters the star tracker and is sequentially reflected by
each mirror before reaching the standard mirror at the entrance
pupil. After reflection by the standard mirror, the beam retraces
its path and returns to the interferometer. The positions and
attitudes of the mirror assemblies are iteratively adjusted to
obtain clear interference fringes. These fringes are subsequently
processed by software to generate a wavefront aberration con-
tour map. By adjusting the position of the standard mirror,
wavefront aberrations corresponding to different fields of view
can be measured, as illustrated in Figs. 16(a)-16(e).
